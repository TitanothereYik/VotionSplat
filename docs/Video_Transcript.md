**[00:01:31] What is Gaussian Splatting?**
Now, what inspired me to get back into this project was Apple's December 2025 release called SHARP: Monocular View Synthesis in less than a second. You just give it a 2D photograph, and in one single pass, a neural network predicts an entire 3D Gaussian representation of that scene. But wait, what actually is a Gaussian splat? In traditional 3D photogrammetry, you would take hundreds or thousands of pictures of your location, and the software then calculates the camera position and the final geometry as a polygon mesh. That's fine for walls and buildings, but for soft things like hair, foliage, or even reflective/transparent surfaces, it will break.

**[00:02:08] How Gaussian Splats Function**
Gaussian splatting throws out polygons entirely. Instead, the scene is a cloud of millions of tiny semi-transparent 3D blobs, technically called ellipsoids. Each one stores a position, size, rotation, and opacity. For color, it uses a math function called spherical harmonics, which is just a really fancy way of saying that the color and brightness change depending on the angle you look at the blob. This is why all the specular and refractive properties of the scene still work using Gaussian splatting. And because there's no ray tracing or shaders, it's super lightweight and runs in real time even on an iPhone. To train a Gaussian splat, an optimizer looks at all the blobs in your scene and adjusts their values until they match every camera position and image in your dataset. This can take a long time, so Apple doing it in like a second is really cool. But there's a catch: Apple's model can only turn a single image into 3D, but I want an entire 3D scene.

**[00:03:01] Failed Initial Experiments (Apple SHARP & MoGe)**
So I had an idea: what if we take a 360-degree panorama, slice it up into multiple views, run Apple SHARP on each of them individually, and stitch them all back together? Turns out it's not that easy. Some angles look really good, but since everything generates individually, you get these really ugly seams. My next idea was to estimate the scene geometry first using a model called MoGe, and then use it to correct and line up the different views by Apple SHARP. I was making some really good progress on this, but I ran into two problems that killed this approach. First of all, it's not flexible enough. The scene looks good from the initial perspective, but once you start moving around, it falls apart because SHARP is designed to preserve the scene and not generate too much new detail. Second, the license. I want to build a tool that is permissive so our audience of small studios and individual CG artists can actually build upon and use it for commercial work.

**[00:03:57] Evaluating Other Models (UniSHARP, Hunyuan, Lyra)**
So I went looking for another solution. UniSHARP is a research project by Insta360, basically the same idea as Apple's project but for 360-degree worlds. It's super fast, but unfortunately, the worlds fall apart really quickly once you start moving. Still a lot of fun to try out, and there's even a Hugging Face demo if you want to try it in your browser. Next, Hunyuan 2.0 looked really promising, but it demands two massive models loaded into memory at once and is designed to run on four GPUs. So... next. Then I tried NVIDIA's Lyra. I actually managed to get it running, but it's 91 GB of checkpoints, Linux-only, and starting it the first time took 6 minutes alone. It's really cool, but not really for consumer hardware.

**[00:04:40] Finding Matrix-3D & Synthetic UE5 Training Data**
But then I followed the white rabbit and found Matrix-3D, which is not a PS2 tie-in game for the movie—it's actually a really smart piece of engineering. You start with a single 360-degree panorama, estimate its depth, and turn it into a rough 3D mesh. When you move a camera through that mesh, it of course breaks and falls apart, because anything the original photo couldn't see becomes a black hole. But then you feed this into the Wan 2.1 video model, which hallucinates all the missing pieces and more. Skywork did two brilliant things here: first, they used a nice mix of real 3D geometry with clever inpainting to maximize consistency; second, they didn't train a whole new model for this—they just trained a lightweight LoRA on top of the Wan 2.1 video model.

**[00:05:26] Unreal Engine Drone Fleet**
But to train that LoRA, they needed a massive dataset of 360-degree video with perfect camera tracking along custom paths. Since that didn't exist, they built it themselves inside Unreal Engine 5. They programmed a virtual 360-degree drone to autonomously fly through 500 different video game environments hundreds of thousands of times. So using the model that they trained, we can now set our own custom path and the model will generate perfect 360-degree videos. What I find strange about this paper is that it dropped in August 2025, but almost nobody built anything around it, maybe because it's not that easy to install. So we rebuilt the entire thing inside ComfyUI.

**[00:06:05] Solving Resolution & Reprojection**
Getting it to work was a bit tricky. The LoRA didn't match ComfyUI's format, and we had to build a few custom nodes for it to work. But once we converted everything, we realized we could use this with a LightX2V speedup model, which massively speeds up render times. We also built our own custom path editor on top of it. This gives you a top-down and side preview of your scene, letting you become a virtual drone pilot. One funny thing is that you can even fly through walls; the model will actually generate a new fitting scene on the other side. But I can instantly tell the resolution is too low. That's because Wan maxes out at 720p video, and imagine stretching this across a full 360-degree field of view. If we trained a Gaussian splat on this data, the structure would work really well, but it's really soft.

**[00:06:47] Reprojecting 8K Textures**
The first thing we tried to improve this was upscaling all the different views using SeedVR, and this helped, but it's so slow and didn't do too much. So I figured it was time for some industrial espionage: I checked out Marble by World Labs, a closed-source model for generating 3D worlds. I was really impressed by how fast it was and how crisp the results were, but the movement area is still really restricted, and there are no working reflections, which is something I really want to keep. But that gave us the idea to reproject the original high-quality renderings onto the MoGe environment. All the drone shots we created start from the same position, and for this position, we have a high-quality image. So what if we just reproject the original high-resolution image back on top of the 360-degree videos at high resolution? We have the camera data and the 3D environment, so in theory, we should know exactly how every single pixel moves. And this worked so well. The transitions between full resolution and Wan are now much smoother, and overall quality improved dramatically.

**[00:11:25] Dataset Creation & Virtual Drone Control**
Now that we have our panorama, drag and drop the dataset creation workflow into ComfyUI. Install missing custom nodes—especially SplatKit and the Mickmumpitz nodes—download all required models, and double-check the model loader group. Upload your image and set an output folder for your scene. Next, scroll to the right and click "Compute Geometry." You get a rough top-down and side preview of your scene. **[00:12:35] Path Editing & Video Generation**
Once you've set the path, click on the save video node and click play to preview the drone flight. You can adjust trajectories to avoid crashing into structures. Scroll down to set up additional drones—for example, one circling a central pond or flying high up in the air before descending. Four drone clips are usually more than enough. When ready, click run to send the data over to the Wan inpainting group, where missing pieces are filled in by the video model and LoRA. The pond retains actual working reflections, so when trained into a Gaussian splat, specular reflections remain intact.

**[00:14:20] Optimizations & COLMAP Export**
Since this workflow is Wan-based, you can apply standard optimizations like SageAttention and Triton to speed up render times. Use the SageAttention patch to avoid rendering black frames. Once generated, the video feeds into the high-res composite node where the original 8K texture is projected back onto the video. Finally, the frames pass into the dataset export node, converting panoramic videos into COLMAP format.

**[00:15:45] Training in Brush & Exploration**
Open your ComfyUI output folder to find your training dataset. Take this dataset to any Gaussian splat trainer—such as Postshot, LichtFeld Studio, or Brush. To use Brush, open the app, click Directory, navigate to `output`, select your scene folder, and click Start. The top window shows the evolving Gaussian point cloud while the bottom window displays the target training frame. As training progresses, you can float around and explore your 3D environment in real time.
